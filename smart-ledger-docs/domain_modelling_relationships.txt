1. Customer ↔ Customer Order
Relationship

One Customer → Many Customer Orders

A customer can place multiple orders.

Customer
   │
   └── 1 : N ──> Customer Order

Example:

Customer A places Order #101, #105 and #120.

But every order belongs to one customer.

Cardinality

Customer 1 : N Order

2. Customer Order ↔ Payment

This is important.

Relationship

One Order → Many Payments

An order may be paid in multiple installments.

Example:

₹1,00,000 order

Advance → ₹30,000
Second payment → ₹40,000
Final payment → ₹30,000

So:

Customer Order
       │
       └── 1 : N ──> Payment

But there's another requirement:

One Payment → Multiple Orders

A customer may pay ₹1,50,000 and that payment could settle:

Order A → ₹50,000
Order B → ₹60,000
Order C → ₹40,000

Therefore, conceptually:

Order ↔ Payment = N : M

This is a case where the relationship itself needs to be represented.

We can later model something like a Payment Allocation concept.

Order ── N : M ── Payment
             │
             └── Allocation

This is an important domain discovery.

3. Supplier ↔ Purchase
Relationship

One Supplier → Many Purchases

A supplier can supply raw silk many times.

Supplier
   │
   └── 1 : N ──> Purchase

Each purchase belongs to one supplier.

4. Purchase ↔ Raw Material

A purchase introduces raw silk into the business.

Conceptually:

Purchase
    │
    └──> Raw Material

If we use the Raw Material Lot concept:

One Purchase → One or Many Raw Material Lots

For example:

Purchase:

100 kg silk

Could become:

Lot A → 60 kg
Lot B → 40 kg

Whether we actually need multiple lots will be finalized later.

5. Raw Material ↔ Customer Order

Raw silk can be allocated against customer demand.

For example:

Customer orders 50 kg raw silk.

The business may reserve 50 kg from available inventory.

So there is a conceptual relationship:

Raw Material
      │
      └──> Allocation ──> Customer Order

This is not necessarily a simple direct relationship because one material lot can be allocated across multiple orders.

Therefore:

Raw Material ↔ Order = potentially N : M

with an allocation concept between them.

6. Raw Material ↔ Production Batch

Raw silk can be consumed by in-house production.

Raw Material
      │
      └──> Production Batch

Example:

100 kg raw silk
→ 80 kg used for production
→ 20 kg remains available

Again, one production batch may consume material from multiple sources.

So conceptually:

Raw Material ↔ Production Batch = N : M

This is another place where a Material Allocation/Consumption relationship may be needed.

7. Production Batch ↔ Finished Warp

A production batch produces finished warp.

Production Batch
       │
       └──> Finished Warp

For example:

80 kg raw silk
→ production
→ 75 kg Kora warp

The production record should allow us to identify:

input weight
output weight
wastage/difference
8. Customer Order ↔ Production Batch

A production batch can be created because of a customer order.

Customer Order
       │
       └──> Production Batch

However, one production batch may potentially satisfy multiple orders.

Example:

Produce 100 kg Kora warp

which is later allocated to:

Customer A → 40 kg
Customer B → 30 kg
Customer C → 30 kg

Therefore, don't assume:

One Order = One Production Batch

The relationship can be:

Order ↔ Production Batch = N : M

with production/allocation logic between them.

9. Worker ↔ Work Record
Relationship

One Worker → Many Work Records

Worker
   │
   └── 1 : N ──> Work Record

Example:

A rolling worker may have work records for:

Monday
Tuesday
Wednesday
etc.
10. Production Batch ↔ Work Record

A work record can belong to a production activity.

Production Batch
       │
       └── 1 : N ──> Work Record

For example:

Production Batch #50:

Worker A → 5 hours
Worker B → 6 hours
Worker C → 4 hours
11. Worker ↔ Worker Payment

Worker payment is different from work performed.

Worker
   │
   ├──> Work Records
   │
   └──> Payments

A worker can have many payments.

A payment may settle multiple work records.

So:

Work Record ↔ Worker Payment = potentially N : M

However, we don't necessarily need to force a detailed allocation model in the MVP if payments are settled periodically.

We'll decide the exact granularity later.

12. Manufacturer ↔ Outsourcing Job
Relationship

One Manufacturer → Many Outsourcing Jobs

Manufacturer
     │
     └── 1 : N ──> Outsourcing Job

A manufacturer may receive many jobs from SmartSilk.

13. Outsourcing Job ↔ Raw Material

An outsourcing job consumes/sends raw silk.

Outsourcing Job
       │
       └──> Raw Material

Example:

100 kg raw silk sent to Manufacturer A.

The system needs to know which material was supplied and how much.

Again, this is essentially a material allocation/consumption relationship.

14. Outsourcing Job ↔ Customer Order

An outsourced job can be associated with a customer order.

Customer Order
       │
       └──> Outsourcing Job

Example:

Customer requests 200 kg Silk warp
→ SmartSilk outsources production
→ Manufacturer produces it.

But one outsourcing job could potentially support multiple orders.

So:

Order ↔ Outsourcing Job = potentially N : M

15. Outsourcing Job ↔ Payment

The manufacturer eventually needs to be paid.

Outsourcing Job
       │
       └──> Payment

The payment could happen later, potentially combining several jobs.

Therefore:

Outsourcing Job ↔ Payment = potentially N : M

This is similar to supplier payments.

16. Customer ↔ Payment

A customer can make many payments.

Customer
   │
   └── 1 : N ──> Payment

But a payment can settle multiple orders.

Therefore:

Customer
   │
   └── 1 : N ──> Payment
                    │
                    └── allocations ──> Orders
17. Supplier ↔ Payment

Same principle.

A supplier can receive many payments.

Supplier
   │
   └── 1 : N ──> Payment

One payment may settle multiple purchases.

Therefore:

Purchase ↔ Payment = N : M

through payment allocation.

18. Financial Account ↔ Financial Transaction

This is one of the most important relationships.

One Financial Account → Many Financial Transactions
Financial Account
       │
       └── 1 : N ──> Financial Transaction

Examples:

Cash

Customer payment
Worker payment
Expense
Supplier payment

Bank Account

Customer UPI
Supplier transfer
Loan received
Bank withdrawal
19. Financial Transaction ↔ Business Entity

A financial transaction can originate from different business events.

For example:

Customer Payment
      ↓
Financial Transaction

or:

Expense
   ↓
Financial Transaction

or:

Loan Received
      ↓
Financial Transaction

or:

Chit Contribution
       ↓
Financial Transaction

Therefore, a Financial Transaction should have a reference to its business reason/source, rather than assuming every transaction is a customer/supplier payment.

20. Loan ↔ Financial Transaction

A loan has multiple financial events.

Example:

Loan
 │
 ├── Loan received
 ├── Interest payment
 ├── Principal payment
 └── Final repayment

Therefore:

One Loan → Many Financial Transactions

21. Chit ↔ Financial Transaction

Similarly:

Chit
 │
 ├── Contribution
 ├── Contribution
 ├── Contribution
 └── Payout

So:

One Chit → Many Financial Transactions

But the Chit has its own business-level records such as contributions, bidding and payout.

22. Expense ↔ Financial Transaction

An expense represents the business reason.

The financial transaction represents the actual money movement.

Expense
   │
   └── 1 : 1 ──> Financial Transaction

For example:

Electricity expense ₹8,000

creates:

Cash/Bank outflow ₹8,000

The exact relationship may later become one-to-many if partial payments are allowed, but for the MVP we can initially treat a normal expense as having one corresponding financial movement.

23. Bill ↔ Customer Order

A sales bill is generated from the underlying sale/order.

Customer Order
      │
      └──> Bill

But:

Bill ≠ Payment

A bill can exist before payment.

24. Purchase ↔ Bill

Supplier purchase bills are associated with purchases.

Purchase
   │
   └──> Purchase Bill

The document provides evidence of the purchase but doesn't itself mean the supplier has been paid.

25. Entity ↔ Documents

Various entities may have supporting documents.

For example:

Purchase → Supplier Bill
Expense  → Receipt
Loan     → Loan Document
Sale     → Customer Bill

Therefore, documents are generally attached to business records, rather than becoming the source of truth.

26. Notification ↔ Business Entity

A notification is generated because something happened or is approaching.

Examples:

Supplier Payable → Supplier Due Notification

Customer Outstanding → Customer Reminder

Loan Due → Loan Notification

Low Stock → Inventory Notification

So:

Notification → references the relevant business entity

27. The Most Important Relationship: Material Flow

For SmartSilk, there is one relationship chain that is more important than almost anything else.

Purchase
   ↓
Raw Silk
   ↓
 ┌───────────────┬──────────────────┐
 ↓               ↓                  ↓
Direct Sale   In-house           Outsourcing
                 ↓                  ↓
              Kora Warp        Finished Warp
                 ↓                  ↓
                 └────────┬─────────┘
                          ↓
                    Customer Order
                          ↓
                    Customer Delivery

This is the physical/material flow of the business.

SmartSilk should eventually be able to answer:

"Where did this silk go?"

For example:

Purchased 500 kg

System should be able to conceptually trace:

100 kg → direct sale
150 kg → in-house production
100 kg → outsourcing
100 kg → still available
50 kg → returned/wastage/discrepancy

This is why material allocation/consumption relationships are important.

29. Three Major Graphs of SmartSilk

This is an important architectural insight.

SmartSilk isn't just one relationship graph.

It has three major interconnected flows:

A. Material Flow
Supplier
   ↓
Purchase
   ↓
Raw Silk
   ↓
Production / Outsourcing / Sale
   ↓
Finished Warp
   ↓
Customer
B. Business Flow
Customer
   ↓
Order
   ↓
Production / Allocation
   ↓
Delivery
   ↓
Bill
C. Financial Flow
Business Event
      ↓
Payment / Financial Transaction
      ↓
Cash / Bank

And these three flows connect.

That's essentially what makes SmartSilk more than a simple bookkeeping application.

One Important Thing We Discovered

While defining relationships, we've discovered that some relationships are too important to remain just relationships.

For example:

Order ↔ Payment

We need to know:

How much of this particular payment was allocated to this particular order?

So we may need:

Payment Allocation

Similarly:

Raw Material ↔ Production

We need to know:

How many kg from this material source were consumed by this production operation?

So we may need:

Material Allocation / Consumption

These aren't necessarily final entities yet. We'll identify such cases formally in the next stages.

4.4 Output

Our preliminary domain relationship model now establishes four major areas:

People

Customer, Supplier, Worker, Manufacturer

Operations

Purchase, Order, Production, Outsourcing, Work

Material

Raw Silk → Production/Outsourcing → Finished Warp

Money

Payment → Financial Transaction → Cash/Bank
