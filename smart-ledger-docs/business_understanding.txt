SmartSilk — Business & Problem Analysis
1. Business Overview
1.1 Business Domain

SmartSilk is intended for a silk yarn trading and silk warping business.

The business primarily involves:

Purchasing raw silk yarn from suppliers.
Selling raw silk yarn directly to customers.
Processing silk yarn into warp.
Producing warp internally.
Outsourcing warping-related manufacturing to other manufacturers when required.
Selling the resulting warp to customers.
Managing customer payments and outstanding amounts.
Managing supplier payments and credit obligations.
Managing business expenses.
Managing investments such as chit funds.
Managing short-term loans when immediate cash is required.

Although accounting is an important part of the system, the broader objective is complete visibility of the business's material flow, sales, payments, expenses, investments and financial position.

2. Business Objective

The current business is operated primarily through manual records, particularly ledger notebooks.

The objective of SmartSilk is to provide a centralized system for tracking the complete business lifecycle, from purchasing silk yarn to processing, selling, receiving payments, paying suppliers, managing expenses, investments and loans.

The long-term objective is not simply to digitize the existing ledger.

It is to allow the business owner to answer questions such as:

How much silk do we currently have?
How much silk is currently being processed?
How much warp has been produced?
How much has been sold?
How much money should we receive from customers?
How much do we owe suppliers?
Which payments are approaching or overdue?
How much money is available?
How much is committed to chit funds?
How much has been borrowed?
How much interest must be paid?
Where is money being lost?
Where is material being lost?
How is the business performing overall?
3. People Involved
3.1 Primary Users

The system is intended primarily for family members involved in the business.

There is currently no requirement for a large external user base.

Therefore, the initial system can be considered a single-business/single-organization system, with a small number of trusted internal users.

3.2 External Parties

Although they are not direct SmartSilk users, the business interacts with:

Silk yarn suppliers
Customers/clients
Other manufacturers/job workers
WhatsApp
Financial institutions/lenders
Chit-fund organizations

These parties are important to the business even though they may not directly operate SmartSilk.

5. Raw Silk Procurement Process

The business purchases silk yarn from suppliers.

Suppliers can be:

Businesses that supply raw silk yarn.
Factories that extract silk from cocoons and supply the resulting silk yarn.

The supplier and material characteristics vary depending on business requirements and demand.

Purchases may happen:

Weekly
Monthly
Based on customer demand

The purchased material may be received together rather than in multiple partial deliveries.

5.1 Supplier Credit

Purchases are generally made on credit.

An advance may sometimes be given, but it is not a fixed rule.

Unlike customers, suppliers have a defined payment period.

The supplier's credit period can vary.

If payment exceeds the agreed period, a penalty may be applicable for each additional day.

Therefore, SmartSilk needs to track:

Purchase
   ↓
Amount payable
   ↓
Credit period
   ↓
Due date
   ↓
Payment
   ↓
Remaining payable
   ↓
Possible overdue
   ↓
Penalty

7. Internal Warp Production

Warp production has two operational stages for tracking purposes.

Stage 1 — Yarn Rolling

The silk yarn is rolled.

During this process, some wastage occurs.

Silk Yarn
   ↓
Rolling
   ↓
Wastage
   ↓
Roll

The business wants to track this because it provides visibility into material loss.

Stage 2 — Warping

The resulting roll is processed using the warp machine.

The warp is produced according to required measurements/specifications.

The business uses measurements related to the type/format of warp and the expected number of sarees that can be produced from it.

The exact format and measurement rules are still to be formally documented.

8. Outsourced Manufacturing / Job Work

The business can also provide silk yarn to other manufacturers.

The process is:

SmartSilk
   │
   ├── Provides silk yarn
   │
   └── Gives manufacturing order
              ↓
        External Manufacturer
              ↓
             Warp
              ↓
        Warp returned

The important distinction is:

The external manufacturer is paid for the manufacturing/labor work, rather than for the silk itself.

The business does not currently treat the silk cost or wastage as the job worker's manufacturing charge because the silk is supplied by the business and the wastage is returned.

Payment to the job worker is based on the weight of the produced warp.

Therefore:

Job Work Payment
=
Warp quantity × agreed manufacturing rate

The exact rate varies and will need to be captured when the business rules are formalized.

9. Customer Orders

Customers place orders primarily through:

Phone calls
WhatsApp

Orders may be:

Single-quantity orders
Bulk orders

There is no fixed order size.

The business may also manufacture regular warp orders during peak seasons based on expected demand.

10. Customer Sales

Customers may purchase:

Raw silk yarn
Finished warp

Customer payment behavior differs from supplier payment behavior.

Customers do NOT have a fixed payment deadline.

A customer may:

Pay an advance.
Pay partially.
Pay several times during a month.
Pay the full amount later.
Pay after several months.

Therefore, customer payment tracking is based primarily on the customer's total outstanding balance.

11. Customer Outstanding Model

This is an important characteristic of the business.

The current practice is not to maintain a strict payment schedule for every order.

Instead:

Total credit given to customer
              ↓
       Customer payments
              ↓
       Current outstanding

For example:

Customer purchases multiple orders
            ↓
      Total credit = X
            ↓
      Payments = Y
            ↓
 Outstanding = X - Y

The customer may later pay an amount that effectively settles an older outstanding order.

The business therefore needs to maintain historical order information while also maintaining the customer's overall outstanding balance.

12. Customer Payment Behavior

A customer may purchase several orders within a month.

For example:

January
 ├── Order A
 ├── Order B
 └── Order C

Payment received
        ↓
     ₹X

February
 ├── Order D
 └── Order E

Payment received
        ↓
     ₹Y

The payment may not correspond to the most recent order.

Therefore, SmartSilk must preserve the history of orders and payments, while the business continues to think in terms of the customer's outstanding amount.

13. Customer Billing

A bill is not necessarily generated immediately after every sale.

The business maintains bills for its own filing.

A customer may request a bill, particularly when they require it for GST/business purposes.

When an order has effectively been fully settled, the corresponding bill can be generated.

The bill may then be:

Retained for business records.
Shared with the customer when requested.

Therefore:

Order
 ↓
Credit
 ↓
Payments over time
 ↓
Order fully settled
 ↓
Bill prepared
 ↓
Customer requests?
 ├── Yes → Share
 └── No  → Keep for filing
14. WhatsApp Requirement

Currently, bills may need to be photographed/copied and sent manually through WhatsApp.

This creates unnecessary manual work.

The desired process is:

Order fully settled
       ↓

       ↓

       ↓

       ↓

The system should eventually reduce the manual process involved in generating and transferring bills.

15. Business Expenses

Business money is also used for various expenses.

The current practice is to maintain an expense ledger.

A major requirement is to distinguish between:

Business expenses

Money spent for operating the business.

Personal drawings

Money taken from business funds for personal purposes.

Both reduce available business cash, but they should be distinguishable for business analysis.

16. Chit Fund Investments

The business invests in multiple chit funds.

Chit payments are made monthly.

The business owner keeps track of these commitments and separates the required money from available business funds.

The chit fund can eventually provide a lump-sum amount.

That lump sum can be used for:

Repaying short-term loans
Paying outstanding business obligations
Supporting business requirements
Other investments

Therefore, the chit fund is not simply a monthly expense.

It represents a recurring financial commitment that can later produce a lump-sum inflow.

17. Short-Term Loans

When available business cash is insufficient for requirements such as:

Purchasing raw silk
Meeting chit-fund commitments
Other urgent business requirements

the business may take short-term loans.

These loans involve interest.

The business therefore needs to track:

Loan
 ↓
Principal
 ↓
Interest obligation
 ↓
Repayment
 ↓
Remaining loan

Chit-fund lump sums may subsequently be used to repay these loans.

This creates an important relationship between:

Cash availability
      ↓
Loan requirement
      ↓
Loan interest
      ↓
Chit maturity/lump sum
      ↓
Loan repayment
18. Current Major Problems
Problem 1 — Manual Ledger Maintenance

Large numbers of transactions are recorded manually in ledger notebooks.

This makes it difficult to:

Search historical transactions.
Understand the current position.
Analyze trends.
Quickly identify outstanding amounts.
Connect related transactions.
Problem 2 — Lack of Overall Business Visibility

The business contains information about:

Purchases
Sales
Payments
Expenses
Job work
Investments
Loans

but the information is primarily maintained as records rather than as an integrated view.

The owner therefore has to mentally combine different records to understand the current state.

Problem 3 — Customer Outstanding Is Difficult to Analyze

Customers can:

Order frequently.
Pay partially.
Pay irregularly.
Pay old outstanding amounts later.

There is no fixed customer payment deadline.

Therefore, manually understanding:

"How much does this customer currently owe us?"

and:

"Which old transactions have effectively been settled?"

can become difficult.

Problem 4 — Supplier Payment Tracking

Supplier credit periods vary.

If payment is delayed beyond the agreed period, penalties may occur.

Manual tracking creates the possibility of:

Missing due dates.
Delaying payments.
Incurring penalties.
Losing visibility of upcoming obligations.
Problem 5 — Material Visibility

The business needs to know:

How much silk was purchased.
How much silk is available.
How much is being processed.
How much is lost as wastage.
How much becomes warp.
How much is sold directly.
How much is outsourced for job work.

Currently, this information is not available as one integrated business view.

Problem 6 — Production Visibility

The warping process involves multiple stages:

Yarn
 ↓
Rolling
 ↓
Wastage
 ↓
Roll
 ↓
Warping
 ↓
Warp

Without structured tracking, it becomes difficult to understand:

What is currently in process.
What has completed rolling.
What has reached warping.
What has been completed.
Where material loss occurs.
Problem 7 — Cash-Flow Visibility

Business money is simultaneously required for:

Raw material purchases.
Supplier payments.
Customer-related operations.
Expenses.
Chit payments.
Loan repayment.

When cash is insufficient, short-term loans may be required.

Therefore, the business needs better visibility into:

Available cash
+
Expected customer receipts
-
Supplier obligations
-
Chit commitments
-
Loan obligations
-
Business expenses
Problem 8 — Investment and Loan Information Is Separate

Chit funds and short-term loans affect the financial position of the business, but they are separate from ordinary sales and purchase transactions.

Without an integrated view, it becomes difficult to understand the overall financial position.

Problem 9 — Manual Bill Sharing

Bills need to be prepared, copied/photographed and sent through WhatsApp.

This becomes repetitive, particularly when customers are in different locations.

21. Long-Term Business Goal

The ultimate objective is:

Complete visibility of the business from raw-material purchase through production and sales to customer payments, supplier payments and overall financial position.

The system should allow the business owner to understand not only what happened, but eventually:

why it happened, where money/material is being lost, and where the business can improve.

This creates the foundation for future analytical and AI capabilities.

22. Future Analytical Capabilities

AI is considered a future improvement, rather than the foundation of the core system.

Potential future capabilities include:

Business performance analysis.
Cash-flow analysis.
Identifying unusual expenses.
Identifying material wastage.
Customer payment behavior analysis.
Supplier payment analysis.
Loan/interest analysis.

The important principle is:

SmartSilk must first create reliable business data before AI can provide reliable analysis.
