Step 7.4 — Database Query & Reporting Design

Now we move from:

“How do we store the data?”

to:

“How do we retrieve meaningful business information from that data?”

This is especially important for SmartSilk because the whole purpose of the system is visibility.

Your father doesn't just need screens saying:

Purchase created successfully.

He needs answers like:

How much silk do I actually have?
How much is outside with manufacturers?
Who owes me money?
Who do I owe?
How much money is available right now?
What orders are still pending?

7.4.1 The Most Important Principle

Don't create database columns for every dashboard number.

For example, don't make:

customer.outstanding_amount
supplier.outstanding_amount
warehouse.current_stock
bank.balance
monthly_profit

and manually update them everywhere.

Instead:

                    SOURCE TRANSACTIONS
                           │
          ┌────────────────┼─────────────────┐
          ▼                ▼                 ▼
      Inventory          Finance          Orders
          │                │                 │
          ▼                ▼                 ▼
      Movements       Transactions       Allocations
          │                │                 │
          └────────────────┼─────────────────┘
                           ▼
                       REPORTING
                           │
                           ▼
                       Dashboard

The dashboard is a view of the underlying truth.

7.4.2 First Business Question — Current Raw Silk Stock

Suppose the movement ledger contains:

Movement	Quantity
Purchase receipt	+500 kg
Production consumption	-100 kg
Direct sale	-50 kg
Return	+20 kg
Outsourcing	-150 kg

Current warehouse raw silk:

500 - 100 - 50 + 20 - 150
= 220 kg

The database doesn't need:

current_raw_silk = 220

The system derives it from movements.

7.4.3 But Stock Needs a Dimension

Simply calculating:

SUM(quantity)

isn't enough.

We need to consider:

product_type
location

For example:

RAW_SILK
WAREHOUSE

is different from:

RAW_SILK
EXTERNAL_WIP

and:

SILK_WARP
FINISHED_STOCK

So conceptually:

Stock(product_type, location)

Example:

Product	Location	Stock
Raw Silk	Warehouse	220 kg
Raw Silk	External WIP	150 kg
Silk Warp	Finished	80 kg
Kora Warp	Finished	45 kg

This gives a much more useful picture.

7.4.4 Inventory Dashboard

The first dashboard section could show:

┌─────────────────────────────────────┐
│ INVENTORY                            │
├─────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Raw Silk              220 kg        │
│ Silk Warp              80 kg        │
│ Kora Warp              45 kg        │
│ Vuda Warp              30 kg        │
│ External WIP           150 kg        │
└─────────────────────────────────────┘

But there's another important question:

Who has the 150 kg external WIP?

So we need a second query grouped by manufacturer.

7.4.5 External WIP by Manufacturer

Example:

Manufacturer	Issued	Received	Current WIP
Manufacturer A	300 kg	250 kg	50 kg
Manufacturer B	200 kg	120 kg	80 kg
Manufacturer C	100 kg	80 kg	20 kg

Total:

150 kg

This is directly useful to your father's real workflow.

He can ask:

"How much silk is currently outside?"

and then:

"With whom?"

7.4.6 Inventory Movement History

Another useful query:

Show all movements for a particular material.

Example:

RAW SILK
----------------------------

Sep 01   Receipt       +300 kg
Sep 03   Production    -100 kg
Sep 04   Outsourcing   -80 kg
Sep 05   Return         +10 kg

This is valuable when someone asks:

"Why does the system say I have only 130 kg?"

You can trace the answer.

This is why movement-based inventory is superior to simply storing a balance.

7.4.7 Customer Outstanding

Now finance.

Suppose:

Order A = ₹50,000
Order B = ₹30,000
Order C = ₹20,000

Total obligation:

₹1,00,000

Customer pays:

₹60,000

Outstanding:

₹40,000

But don't calculate:

customer.outstanding

as a manually maintained field.

Instead:

Order obligations
        -
Payment allocations
        =
Outstanding
7.4.8 Why Payment Allocation Is So Important

Imagine:

Customer owes:

Order A = ₹30,000
Order B = ₹40,000
Order C = ₹50,000

Payment:

₹60,000

Allocation:

Order A → ₹30,000
Order B → ₹30,000

Outstanding:

Order B → ₹10,000
Order C → ₹50,000

Total:

₹60,000

Without allocations, you know the customer paid ₹60,000 but don't know which obligations were settled.

7.4.9 Customer Outstanding Dashboard

Example:

Customer	Outstanding	Oldest Due
Customer A	₹85,000	18 days
Customer B	₹42,000	7 days
Customer C	₹1,20,000	31 days

Now the owner can prioritize collection.

This is more useful than merely showing:

Total receivables = ₹2,47,000
7.4.10 Supplier Payables

Same principle in reverse.

Suppose:

Purchase 1 = ₹1,00,000
Purchase 2 = ₹80,000
Purchase 3 = ₹60,000

Payments:

₹1,20,000

Outstanding:

₹1,20,000

But because the business pays oldest-first, the allocation might be:

Purchase 1 → ₹1,00,000
Purchase 2 → ₹20,000

Remaining:

Purchase 2 → ₹60,000
Purchase 3 → ₹60,000

This allows SmartSilk to answer:

Which supplier bills are actually overdue?

7.4.11 Supplier Due Report

Example:

Supplier	Outstanding	Due Date	Status
Supplier A	₹75,000	Sep 08	OVERDUE
Supplier B	₹40,000	Sep 15	UPCOMING
Supplier C	₹1,20,000	Sep 20	UPCOMING

This directly feeds your reminder system.

7.4.12 Cash Position

Cash isn't the same thing as profit.

Suppose:

Cash = ₹1,00,000
Bank = ₹2,00,000

Available money:

₹3,00,000

But the business may also have:

Supplier payable = ₹2,50,000
Customer receivable = ₹4,00,000

So:

Current money = ₹3,00,000

doesn't mean:

Profit = ₹3,00,000

This distinction is central to SmartSilk.

7.4.13 Financial Account Balance

For each financial account:

Balance
=
Money In
-
Money Out

Example:

SBI
+₹5,00,000
-₹2,00,000
-₹50,000
+₹1,00,000 transfer

Balance:

₹3,50,000

But the ₹1,00,000 transfer from another SmartSilk account shouldn't be counted as business revenue.

That's why account_transfers matter.

7.4.14 Financial Position Dashboard

A useful dashboard might show:

┌──────────────────────────────────────┐
│ MONEY POSITION                       │
├──────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Cash                 ₹1,00,000       │
│ SBI                  ₹2,50,000       │
│ HDFC                 ₹75,000         │
│ UPI                  ₹25,000         │
├──────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Total Available      ₹4,50,000       │
└──────────────────────────────────────┘

Then separately:

Receivables             ₹4,20,000
Payables                ₹2,80,000

This gives a much more realistic business picture.

7.4.15 Pending Orders

Orders should be queried by status.

Example:

status IN (
    CONFIRMED,
    IN_PROGRESS
)

Dashboard:

Order	Customer	Weight	Due	Status
ORD-101	A	100 kg	Sep 12	IN_PROGRESS
ORD-102	B	80 kg	Sep 13	CONFIRMED
ORD-103	C	150 kg	Sep 15	CONFIRMED

This can then be compared with inventory.

7.4.16 A More Valuable Query

Instead of simply:

"How many orders are pending?"

SmartSilk can eventually answer:

"Do we have enough silk to fulfill pending orders?"

Example:

Available raw silk = 200 kg

Pending order requirements:
Order A = 80 kg
Order B = 70 kg
Order C = 100 kg

Total = 250 kg

Therefore:

Shortfall = 50 kg

This is directly aligned with how your father actually thinks about the business.

7.4.17 Purchase Decision Support

Now SmartSilk becomes more than bookkeeping.

It can show:

Current raw silk              200 kg
Pending production requirement 250 kg
Shortfall                      50 kg

And if the purchase threshold is:

50 kg

the system can flag:

Purchase planning required.

This is a derived business insight, not a manually entered value.

7.4.18 Production Performance

Production batch:

Input = 100 kg
Output = 92 kg
Wastage = 8 kg

Yield:

92 / 100 × 100
= 92%

The database stores:

input
output
wastage

The report derives:

yield_percentage

This allows:

Batch A → 92%
Batch B → 95%
Batch C → 89%

Later you can analyze:

Which production type has higher wastage?

7.4.19 Outsourcing Performance

For an outsourcing job:

Issued = 200 kg
Received = 185 kg
Difference = 15 kg

We can derive:

recovery = 92.5%

Now compare manufacturers:

Manufacturer	Issued	Received	Recovery
A	500 kg	470 kg	94%
B	400 kg	350 kg	87.5%
C	300 kg	285 kg	95%

This eventually becomes useful for:

Outsource vs own production decisions.

7.4.20 Worker Cost Report

Suppose:

Worker A
100 kg × ₹30 = ₹3,000

Worker B
80 kg × ₹35 = ₹2,800

Monthly worker cost:

₹5,800

You can group by:

worker
work_type
month
production_batch

This supports your future production costing.

7.4.21 Monthly Expense Report

Example:

Category	September
Transport	₹12,000
Electricity	₹8,000
Workers	₹45,000
Maintenance	₹6,000
Other	₹4,500
Total	₹75,500

This comes from expenses, not from arbitrary account withdrawals.

7.4.22 Personal Drawings Must Be Separate

Suppose your father takes:

₹20,000

for personal use.

Money has left the business account.

But it should not automatically become:

Business expense = ₹20,000

Instead:

Financial Transaction
direction = OUT
classification = PERSONAL_DRAWING

Then:

Cash position ↓ ₹20,000

but:

Operating expense

doesn't increase by ₹20,000.

This is essential for meaningful future profitability analysis.

7.4.23 P&L Is Different

A basic conceptual model:

Revenue
 -
Cost of Goods / Production
 -
Operating Expenses
 =
Profit

But we should not build a sophisticated accounting engine for MVP.

Instead, SmartSilk can initially produce:

Basic business performance
Sales / recognized revenue
- material/production costs
- worker costs
- operating expenses
= estimated operating result

with clear documentation that it is a management report, not a formal statutory accounting system.

This distinction is important because your father's current business process isn't maintained as formal double-entry accounting.

7.4.24 Current Position vs Profitability

This should become one of SmartSilk's major design concepts.

Current position

Answers:

What do I have and what do I owe right now?

Cash
Bank
Inventory
Receivables
Payables
External WIP
Pending commitments
Profitability

Answers:

Did the business actually make money?

Revenue
Costs
Expenses
Profit

These are related but not interchangeable.

7.4.25 Dashboard Architecture

Don't make the frontend calculate everything.

Instead:

Kotlin UI
   │
   ▼
Dashboard API
   │
   ├── Inventory service
   ├── Receivables query
   ├── Payables query
   ├── Finance query
   ├── Orders query
   └── Production query
          │
          ▼
       PostgreSQL

The backend owns the business calculations.

The frontend displays them.

7.4.26 Reporting Queries vs Transaction Queries

This is another useful distinction.

Transaction query

Used during business operation:

"Create payment."

Needs:

validation
transaction
locking
consistency
Reporting query

Used for analysis:

"Show all overdue suppliers."

Needs:

efficient joins
indexes
aggregation
potentially read-only transaction

Don't mix these responsibilities.

7.4.27 Should We Create Database Views?

Yes, potentially.

For example:

customer_outstanding_view
supplier_outstanding_view
inventory_summary_view
financial_account_balance_view

A database view is essentially a reusable query.

Conceptually:

material_movements
        ↓
inventory_summary_view
        ↓
Dashboard

This can make reporting cleaner.

But don't create a view for every screen.

Use views when a calculation is:

reused
complex
logically stable
7.4.28 Materialized Views

A normal view calculates when queried.

A materialized view stores the result.

Example:

inventory_daily_summary

could potentially be materialized later.

But for MVP:

Don't use materialized views unless actual performance requires them.

PostgreSQL + proper indexes should be enough initially.

7.4.29 Reporting API Design

Instead of exposing raw SQL results everywhere, the backend can provide business-oriented endpoints.

For example:

GET /api/dashboard/summary

GET /api/inventory/summary
GET /api/inventory/external-wip

GET /api/customers/outstanding
GET /api/suppliers/payables

GET /api/finance/accounts
GET /api/finance/cash-position

GET /api/orders/pending

GET /api/reports/monthly-expenses
GET /api/reports/production

The exact REST structure will be finalized in Step 8 — API & Backend Design.

7.4.30 One Query Can Support Multiple Screens

For example:

customer_outstanding

could power:

Dashboard
Customer list
Customer detail
Payment screen
Reports
Notifications

This is why reporting should be designed centrally rather than calculating the same thing separately in every UI screen.

7.4.31 The Query Design Rule

For every important business metric, ask:

1. What is the source of truth?

Example:

Inventory → material_movements
Money → financial_transactions
Payments → payments
Order obligations → order_items
2. What calculation produces the metric?

Example:

Outstanding =
obligation - allocations
3. Can it become inconsistent?

If yes, don't store it as an independently editable field.

4. What index does the query need?

Optimize after identifying the actual query pattern.

Business Question	Source	Derived Result

Supplier owed?	Purchases + allocations	Payable

7.4.34 Why This Matters for Your AI Module

Later, when we build the AI financial advisory, the AI shouldn't directly query random tables and invent conclusions.

Instead:

PostgreSQL
     ↓
Validated reporting/query layer
     ↓
Structured business metrics
     ↓
AI reasoning
     ↓
Explanation / recommendation

For example:

Raw silk = 220 kg
Pending requirement = 250 kg
Supplier payable = ₹2.8L
Cash available = ₹4.5L
External WIP = 150 kg

AI can then say:

"Your immediate raw-silk requirement exceeds available stock by approximately 30 kg, while ₹2.8 lakh is currently payable to suppliers."

The AI is reasoning over validated facts, not becoming the source of financial truth.
